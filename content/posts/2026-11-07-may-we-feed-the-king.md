*John Updike’s *Memories of the Ford Administration* is not a classic, it’s not his best work, and it’s not even realy about the Ford Administration. But its 19th-century history parallels Rebecca Perry’s medieval history so closely that it helps illustrate what makes her book so special.*

## The Narrators 

>The point, though, I said, the whole point was that I needed a safeguard against certainty—I could not be permitted to create a definitive story for my King, setting him firmly in a history bent to my desires.
<cite>Rebecca Perry, <em>May We Feed the King</em></cite>

The narrator of Rebecca Perry’s Booker shortlisted novel <i>May We Feed the King</i> is not a storyteller. She curates archival details and makes exacting decisions about which individual details most honestly represent the object of her inquiry. Her current assignment is a palace that has hosted centuries of England’s kings and queens. The props she selects for this scene will live in the minds of site visitors long after their anachronistic vehicles pull out of the cordoned parking lot, so she has a responsibility to deposit honesty in the royal bedchambers. "The chair pushed under the desk, the candles startlingly upright, burnt down a third. Peeking from the pages of a book of prayers is a pressed copper birch leaf, used to mark the page." 

The book, the leaf, the chair, the candle, each is a data point from which a revealing graph may be constructed. So the narrator’s peculiar task is to tell the Truth, and Nothing But the Truth, but the Whole Truth is pie-in-the-sky. The curator is Valentine in Tom Stoppard’s <i>Arcadia</i>, feeding gamebook statistics about hunted grouse into a computer capable of synthesizing them into supportable patterns. These are not just the governing principles of the curator’s palace project. They are the North Star of Perry’s elliptical story. Perry defies storytelling conventions, but to what end? And to what degree of Booker-season achievement?

>He spoke, though less than half a generation younger than I, with the brash authority of the New Thinking; gray heads bowed and made way, in this quaint institution devoted to the interim care of well-bred, well-off girls en route from their fathers to their husbands, for “studies” whose ideal texts were the diaries, where they could be found, of black female slaves. He was active, dynamic, persuasive, and committed. He took teaching too seriously, it seemed to me—as a species of political activity, as an opportunity for the exercise of power, even while decrying the white male power of bygone generations.
<cite>John Updike, <em>Memories of the Ford Administration</em></cite>

The narrator of John Updike’s <i>Memories of the Ford Administration</i> is Alfred Clayton, a historian who can’t resist drawing patterns from his data. He shares the curator’s distrust of certainty, which swaggers presumptuously in the historical analysis of his revisionist colleague Brent, whom Clayton is openly cuckolding. That indiscreet data point is inextricable from Clayton’s curation of the life of America’s 15th President, James Buchanan, which is itself inextricable from the ethos that surrounds the 38th President, Gerald Ford. Pattern is drawn from pattern with nary a chapter break, and no data point goes uninterpreted. "I can't finish the damn Buchanan book is that I have too much to say," is simultaneous pillow talk with Brent’s bride and part of the book Clayton can’t finish. "And yet nothing really new. Just the old facts, churned up again."

Even without charting voluminous data visualizations, the data points that comprise <i>May We Feed the King</i> are plotted in striking juxtaposition to those from <i>Memories of the Ford Administration</i>. If we sidestep overweening certainty, perhaps charting those alignments may yield answers to the questions posed above.

## The Subjects

The curator needs to choose a King for her exhibitions to focus on. She considers the most reviled King, known as the "Apple King" because he lobbed fruit at his opponents. Instead, she chooses another problematic King, one who lobbed raw fruit at his enemies. The King she chooses has a legacy that is at once more complex and unpopular in his own time.

<blockquote>Elsewhere, in the dark of corridors and in the shade of the gardens, whispers begin to circulate thus:<br>
		<i>The King is seeking to absolve himself of his duties.</i>:<br>
			<i>He makes inquiries to people who owe him no loyalty. Idiot man.</i><br>
		<i>No doubt he has designs to make off in plain sight, to great fanfare, on a horse decked in gold cloth.</i><br> 
<i>No doubt he thinks he can get away with anything.</i><br>
<i>By what authority? By what authority does he believe he may refuse the throne?<i>
<cite>Rebecca Perry, <i>May We Feed the King</i></cite></blockquote>

Perry, a poet with finalist credentials for the T.S. Eliot Prize, is strutting her stuff here. Her debut novel examines this unpopular King with the ambiguous vocabulary of poetry, where language is simultaneously pregnant with meaning and confoundingly difficult to penetrate. 

His advisors murmur dissension. Rebel forces cluster on the horizon. Perry resists the temptation to issue statements of authorial approval or condemnation of her iconoclastic monarch. But her portrayal is endowed with implicit compassion, even admiration.

Alfred Clayton’s chosen subject is the consensus choice for the worst president in U.S. history. His unwillingness to hold the South accountable for slavery or for their secessionist rumblings has resulted in last-place finishes in each of the last four election-year Presidential Historians Surveys hosted by C-SPAN. In 2021, he was the only president to rank below Donald Trump. Clayton’s assessment of Buchanan betrays characteristic nuance, but he has a strong impulse to vindicate Lincoln’s predecessor from the dustbin of history.

>I volunteered, “I probably don’t want to finish it. I’m scared of being separated from him.” “From Buchanan?” she said, in genuine surprise. We could still surprise each other; that was nice. “Yes. I love him,” I said. …  “Is he lovable?” she sensibly asked. “Not very,” I admitted, then backtracked, “but yes, very. He was stiff and conscientious and cautious. His Presidential addresses are so dry you could learn to hate him. But then you don’t, you get to feel a mind underneath the words, making sense, trying to pull off a balancing act.”
<cite>John Updike, <em>Memories of the Ford Administration</em></cite>

## The Pacifistm

The King is not a natural leader. He lacks not only the certainty of his brothers but also their bloodlust. He mourns the loss of men he sentences to death at the behest of his underlings. Even when forces mass on the borders of his reign, the King wants to make peace with them. 

Buchanan has long been an ally of the South, and as President he resists the belliclose demands of his Southern allies. 

>These men had been his friends and political allies a few weeks ago, solid congenial men with whom, save for a few trifling matters such as the sacred status of slavery, he had no disagreements. It now seemed that that had been an illusion. These men were willing to sink his Presidency and douse Anderson and his troops in blood. 
<cite>John Updike, <em>Memories of the Ford Administration</em></cite>

Clayton is determined to rehabilite his favorite president> "History loves blood," he writes. "It loves the great blood-spillers. Poor Buchanan was ahead of his time, trying to bring mankind up a notch, out of the blood."

## The Sexual Mysteries

The King’s subjects don’t know what to make of his sexual appetites, which often spill out in public but which never yield an heir. His relationship with his Queen is burdensomely complex. She has had a lover since before they were married, and he seems to approve of their unofficial union. But on sleepless nights, he begs her to act as a sexual sedative and she obliges. Then, a rumor aemerges that might explain it all.

>Rumor is that, while innocently walking through the gardens, the Queen’s maid-of-honor chanced upon a window—a beautiful window shaped like an almond— and framed in that window was the young Attendant leaning back upon a table, cock in the mouth of the King, who knelt before him on the floor. What was she to do, in her shock, but run to the other ladies and tell them what she had seen in great detail—how the young Attendant had his eyes closed and his mouth open while the King was looking up at him, imploring.
<cite>Rebecca Perry, <i>May We Feed the King</i></cite>

Buchanan is the picture of Victorian respectability, even across the Atlantic from Victoria, but his love life, too, prompts whispers, giggles, and pointed fingers. Buchanan is the only U.S. president who never married. Many modern observers prefer to explain Buchanan’s marital status with the love that dare not speak its name. After all, he lived for several years with another man, the future Vice President William Rufus King. Updike is open to the idea, but his assessment in the novel hardly waves any rainbow flags.

>American gays, having seized as theirs Whitman, Melville, and Henry James, among our crusty, straight-lipped Presidents must be satisfied with Buchanan, our only never-married chief executive, who in the heat of 1860 was characterized thus by a calumnious but not unpoetic pro-Douglas paper: Mr. B. has a shrill, almost female voice, and wholly beardless cheeks.
<cite>John Updike, <em>Memories of the Ford Administration</em></cite>

Meanwhile, Updike builds a convincing case that Buchanan’s early engagment to a young woman named Ann Coleman, who passed away shortly after sending him a haughty Dear John letter that condemned his friendships with other women. 
 
## The Arguments

Perry is a newly minted novelist, which means that her prose has not been flattened by the need to resolve reality and generate authentic dialogue. As a novelist, Perry is a poet: She cares more for ornamented ambiguity than she does for realistic resolution. As a historian, Perry is also a poet. Her historiographic approach begins with accumulated data points and mounts into interpretation. In a general way that aligns her with no particular established theory. In another, Perry’s approach is as bottom-up as that of Howard Zinn, the revered author of <i>The People’s History’</i>. Her focus on the King’s attendant recalls Zinn’s preference for everyday accounts of history over those of Great Men. 

Updike neologizes the Zinn mystique as "New Thinking," and his narrator claims Frank supporters are plundering slave diaries instead of probing the beliefs of the marginalized. But his main objection to Mueller roots that character in the "Dark Art" of deconstruction, an art that Updike associates with lost faith and superficial politicization.   

Still, Perry is not afraid to espouse her own coherent historiographic philosophy.

>His great gift to us—or at least it has been a gift to me—is the very lack of an ending, that could drive a historian mad. What a great—pardon my language—”fuck you” to everyone who tries to manhandle ambiguity into order, to knock out the air. To the people that have since pinned him down so cruelly and unambiguously on this page.
<cite>Rebecca Perry, <i>May We Feed the King</i></cite>

Collect the data and eventually the graph emerges, in poetic language illuminated by meaning the author need not explain further.
