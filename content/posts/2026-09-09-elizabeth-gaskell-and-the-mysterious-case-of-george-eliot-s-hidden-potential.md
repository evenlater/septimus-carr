Like any good detective, I started by reconstructing the sequence of events. I would have cracked the case sooner if I had read Elizabeth Gaskell’s _Cranford_ and George Eliot’s _Scenes of a Clerical Life_ together. But in the beginning, I didn’t know there was anything to solve.
 
Last Christmas, I found the first story in _Scenes of a Clerical Life_ confoundingly difficult to latch onto.  Eliot had already lodged herself firmly in the upper echelons of my literary regard on the strength of _Middlemarch_ and _Felix Holt, the Radical_ and several BBC adaptations. But “The Sad Fortunes of the Reverend Amos Barton,” the opening entry in her three-story 1858 debut about ministers in a small-town church, felt dry and awkward compared with her later works. You could see her great mind at work, but the piece was dramatically inert.
 
Then, midway through its successor, “Mr. Gilfil’s Love Story,” the real George Eliot suddenly showed up on the pages before me. It felt as if someone had just whispered to Eliot the advice every writer learns in Creative Writing 101, about the advantage of showing rather than telling. She starts writing dialogue. Her psychological brushwork gains nuance. The events bear weighty stakes; they are not just thinly veiled scaffolds for her moral insights.      
 
A few months later, I was surprised at how easily I was drawn into the richly layered BBC mini-series _North & South_, an adaptation of a capstone 1855 work by the novelist everyone calls “Mrs. Gaskell.” Wanting more but hesitant to allot much of my precious reading time, I devoted a week in July to her slimmest offering. That would be _Cranford,_ the episodic 1853 novella about a small town populated almost entirely by women.
 
A few pages in, it became screamingly clear that the two books presented an ideal essay pairing: two short, episodic works on Victorian provincial life, both published by women authors as periodical serials five years apart. If anything, they were too closely related. Searches on Google and JSTOR revealed how well-trodden the territory was.
 
But the most striking thing about reading _Cranford_ was my instinct to compare each step of the way with the experience of reading _Scenes of a Clerical Life._ In the early chapters, I was captivated by the charm and originality of Gaskell’s conceit and the confidence of her craft. But as I read on, her devices began to feel transparent, tedious, and twee. It was a direct reversal of the experience I’d had with _Scenes of a Clerical Life._ Now the mystery was staring me in the face: What happened to Eliot as a writer between her first and second stories that enabled her to outshine her more accomplished peer? By examining the sequence of the two books side by side in three distinct alternating chunks, and after engaging in a little external research, a surprising explanation began to emerge.        

##<em>Cranford</em> Chapters 1-5 Versus “The Sad Fortunes of the Reverend Amos Barton”
 
Don’t take my word for it; follow along at home. Read the first five chapters of _Cranford,_ halting at the climactic words of chapter five: “Poor Peter!” Take a moment to savor the story’s timeless charm. Wipe away the inevitable tear conjured by the regressive gender distinctions made when Peter is abused for cross-dressing. Admire the artful blend of comedy and pathos when Captain Brown’s life is taken by the very railroad the ladies resist with Luddite fervor—while he reads their least favorite author, Charles Dickens—and give into the melodramatic excess when his daughter dies shortly thereafter.
 
Then, put down Gaskell and pick up Eliot’s debut as a fiction writer. Read the first entry of her trilogy of short stories, “The Sad Fortunes of the Reverend Amos Barton.” See if its relentlessly expository style doesn’t make you long for Gaskell’s practiced craft as a storyteller even as you admire Eliot’s essayistic wisdom. Marvel at the clumsiness of her working-class accents (the worst feature of her writing all the way through _Middlemarch_): “He’s al’ys a-findin’ faut wi’ him, an’ a-poundin’ him for nothin’.” Admit it: at this stage, Eliot’s writing is a bone-dry slog. You prefer Mrs. Gaskell.

> “Be not a baker if your head be made of butter,” says a homely proverb, which, being interpreted, may mean, let no woman rush into print who is not prepared for the consequences.  … By a peculiar thermometric adjustment, when a woman’s talent is at zero, journalistic approbation is at the boiling pitch; when she attains mediocrity, it is already at no more than summer heat; and if ever she reaches excellence, critical enthusiasm drops to the freezing point.  Harriet Martineau, Currer Bell [Charlotte Brontë], and Mrs. Gaskell have been treated as cavalierly as if they had been men.  
<cite>George Eliot, “Silly Novels by Lady Novelists.” Written in the summer of 1856 for <em>The Westminster Review.</em></cite>
 
The above quotation illustrates the state of things on September 22, 1856, when Eliot first tried her hand at fiction. A month from her 46th birthday, Mrs. Gaskell was both a mother of four and already an established bestselling\* author. Gaskell’s milieu was the “social novel,” a genre defined by concern for the poor in a time when technology was reshaping the economy at the expense of the working man. Her first effort, _Mary Barton,_ about residents of industrial Manchester, was so successful that it caught the attention of the social novel’s most prominent practitioner, Dickens himself. The _Oliver Twist_ author asked her to write something for his weekly magazine _Household Words_, and that “something” was to be _Cranford_, conceived as a one-off nostalgia piece about Gaskell’s provincial hometown. Dickens wanted more, and in 1853 it was published as a novel.   

Two years later, _Cranford_ was a TBR\* purchase for Mary Ann Evans, the 36-year-old editor of a radical quarterly journal. Evans had just completed a caustic literary essay titled “Silly Novels by Lady Novelists” that condemned the median feminine novelist for mistaking “vagueness for depth, bombast for eloquence, and affectation for originality.” Bold words for a woman who had not yet ponied up a single word of fiction. Privately, Evans doubted that she had the stuff to back her critical bravado. “As the years passed on I lost any hope that I should ever be able to write a novel,” she confessed in a journal entry about this period titled “How I Came to Write Fiction.” But Evans was not timid about expressing her formidable mind. As a teenager she had permanently strained relations with her devout father (and the surrounding community) by refusing to pretend she believed in Christ’s divinity. Then, as if that weren’t enough scandal for a lifetime, Evans effectively raised her middle finger to stuffy Victorian society by shacking up with a married man.
 
One would not expect the whimsical _Cranford_ to appeal to a writer with a resume of such uncompromising seriousness. But perhaps her admiration for the political gumption of _Mary Barton,_ whose author she had explicitly exempted from the charge of writing “silly novels,” incentivized her purchase of the cheap edition, which then languished on her shelf for a year. Or perhaps it is folly to pigeonhole a woman of Evans’s complexity as too serious or caustic for anything, let alone a captivating novella about the tendency of respectable ladies to bar the door against the march of progress. Perhaps it is always folly to try to shove people into neat boxes in your mind. “It is so much easier to make up your mind that your neighbour is good for nothing,” wrote Evans that fall in her first foray into fiction, “than to enter into all the circumstances that would oblige you to modify that opinion.”
 
That gem of a quotation is the bighearted thesis of “The Sad Fortunes of the Reverend Amos Barton.” In 2026, it deals a shattering blow to the cancel culture mindset. In 1856, the narrative that supported it, about the moral hysteria provoked in a small town by a more-or-less innocent friendship between a pastor and a countess, was soon to persuade a religiously conservative publisher. Evans’s live-in lover, George Lewes, shopped the story to editor John Blackwood as the work of a male friend named George Eliot. The staunch Tory sent back a detailed assessment of “Amos Barton.” He was wary at first (“I hate anything of a sneer at real religious feeling as cordially as I despise anything like cant”) but praised the writer’s “humor and pathos” and concluded, “If the author is a new writer, I beg to congratulate him on being worthy of the honors of print and pay.”


But Eliot’s difficulty shaking the didactic voice she’d developed in years of nonfiction writing and in recent essays like “Silly Novels” still bleeds through every line of “Amos Barton.” She anticipated the challenge long before she set pen to paper. “I always thought I was deficient in dramatic power, both of construction and dialogue,” she told her journal. Her first draft of the story was “pure description.” That flaw that did not escape Blackwood’s notice. “The author falls into the error of trying too much to explain the characters of his actors by description,” he wrote, “instead of allowing them to evolve in the action of the story.”  

 
\*TBR = To Be Read
\* My loose use of the term to mean “financially successful” is deliberately anachronistic, although it was technically coined as far back as 1797 by Isaac d’Israeli, the father of novelist/pol Benjamin Disraeli.

##<em>Cranford</em> Chapters 6-11 Versus “Mr. Gilfil’s Love Story”

Task: Return to _Cranford_ and read its middle section, ensconcing yourself in Gaskell’s leisurely world-building. Don’t stop until the close of the 10th chapter and its suggestion that long-lost “Poor Peter” might be alive after all. Read Eliot’s second entry, “Mr. Gilfil’s Love Story,” then compare and contrast. Even in the slow opening exposition about the passing of a sadsack minister, can’t you feel Eliot closing the gap? Don’t the pages ripple by in mounting acceleration in the ensuing flashback, a gripping cross-class love quadrangle? The authors seem nose-and-nose as they circle the same compassionate spiritualism. In an exchange about colonial India, the Unitarian Gaskell has a Christian woman affirm religious pluralism:


>“I was near one of the native temples, and I went into it with my baby to thank God for His great mercy; for it seemed to me that where others had prayed before to their God, in their joy or in their agony, was of itself a sacred place.”
<cite>Mrs. Brown in Elizabeth Gaskell’s <em>Cranford</em></cite>

Not to be outdone, the agnostic Eliot enlists an objective deity as evidence that humans are not defined by their worst impulses:

>“We mean to do wicked things that we never could do, just as we mean to do good or clever things that we never could do. Our thoughts are often worse than we are, just as they are often better than we are. And God sees us as we are.”
<cite>Maynard Gilfil in George Eliot’s <em>Scenes of a Clerical Life</em>

As the lap draws to a close, however, Gaskell’s pacing seems lackadaisical while Eliot ratchets up the suspense. For the first time, the fledgling author edges past the cagey vet.

Herein lies the mystery I laid out at the outset of this essay: How did Eliot pull of this transformation? Certainly, talent played a significant role; a writer of Eliot’s gifts was sure to find her voice eventually. Moreover, it’s not like she came to fiction unpracticed in the art of putting words together. She’d been a professional writer for years. But so much progress in so little time? “Amos Barton” was begun at the end of September. She started on “Mr. Gilfil’s Love Story” Christmas Day. Astronomers often discover the presence of a celestial body by observing its impact on the objects around it, like Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel’s deduction in 2000 that stars hurtling around an empty patch in space were driven by a supermassive black hole. Eliot’s startling turnaround is evidence of some unseen catalyzing force. But what?

>Since I came from Manchester to London I have had the greatest compliment paid me I ever had in my life. I have been suspected of having written ‘Adam Bede’. I have hitherto denied it; but I really think, that as you want to keep your real name a secret, it would be very pleasant for me to blush acquiescence. Will you give me leave? Well! if I had written Amos Barton, Janet's Repentance and Adam Bede I should neither be to have nor to hold with pride and delight in myself -- so I think it is very well I have not.
<cite>Elizabeth Gaskell, letter to Mr. “Gilbert Elliott,” June 3, 1859

The correspondence between the two women, initiated by Gaskell three years after the fact, is not merely historical color too enchanting to omit. The letters crack the mystery. In June, Gaskell praises _Scenes of a Clerical Life_ and Eliot’s subsequent social novel _Adam Bede._ In November, Eliot writes an effusive reply, thanking Gaskell for sending “the only sort of help I care to have,” that is, recognition from “one of the minds” most singularly capable of understanding her experience and judging her output as a writer. Then, the smoking gun.

> I had indulged the idea that if my books turned out to be worth much, you would be among my willing readers; for I was conscious, while the question of my power was still undecided for me, that my feeling towards Life and Art had some affinity with the feeling which had inspired "Cranford" and the earlier chapters of "Mary Barton." That idea was brought the nearer to me, because I had the pleasure of reading Cranford for the first time in 1857, when I was writing the "Scenes of Clerical Life[.]" … I like to tell you these slight details because they will prove to you that your letter must have a peculiar value for me, and that I am not expressing vague gratitude towards a writer whom I only remember vaguely as one who charmed me in the past. … I fancy, as long as we live,we all need to know as much as we can of the good our life has been to others.
<cite>George Eliot, letter to Elizabeth Gaskell, November 11, 1859

The sudden vivid animation of “Mr. Gilfil’s Love Story,” its agonizingly authentic dialogue and riveting dramatic pacing, are the hurtling stars that point to a supermassive black hole. The black hole, Eliot reveals here, is _Cranford_ itself.

Consider the evidence: Eliot’s own journals testify that the only component of fiction with which she felt fully “at ease” was description. Her first reader, Blackwood, flags that component exactly as the strength of “Amos Barton” (“the descriptions are humorous and good”) while cautioning of overreliance on that technique ( “trying too much to explain the characters of his actors by description”). This was the very problem I had with “Amos Barton” in December 2025: its “relentlessly expository style.”

Before reading _Cranford_, Eliot worried that she had no knack for writing conversation (“deficient in … dialogue”). Blackwood again confirms the deficiency. A few months later, she reads lively exchanges like this:

<blockquote>‘And how came Miss Matilda not to marry him?’ asked I.<br>
‘Oh, I don’t know. She was willing enough, I think; but you know Cousin Thomas would not have been enough of a gentleman for the Rector, and Miss Jenkyns.’<br>
‘Well! but they were not to marry him,’ said I, impatiently.<br>
‘No; but they did not like Miss Matty to marry below her rank. You know she was the Rector’s daughter, and somehow they are related to Sir Peter Arley: Miss Jenkyns thought a deal of that.’<br>
‘Poor Miss Matty!’ said I.<br>
<cite>Elizabeth Gaskell, <en>Cranford</em></blockquote>

And suddenly she is capable of writing exchanges like this:

<blockquote>‘You seem to be on very intimate terms with Miss Sarti.’<br>
‘With Tina? oh yes; she has always been the pet of the house, you know. We have been quite brother and sister together.’<br>
‘Sisters don’t generally colour so very deeply when their brothers approach them.’<br>
‘Does she colour? I never noticed it. But she’s a timid little thing.’<br>
‘It would be much better if you would not be so hypocritical, Captain Wybrow. I am confident there has been some flirtation between you. Miss Sarti, in her position, would never speak to you with the petulance she did last night, if you had not given her some kind of claim on you.’<br>
<cite>George Eliot, “Mr. Gilfil’s Love Story,” <em>Scenes of a Clerical Life</em></cite></blockquote> 

In his book _The Provincial Fiction of Mitford, Gaskell, and Eliot,_ Kevin A. Morrison comes to a similar conclusion. He notes that Mary Russell Mitford’s provincial storytelling in the novel _Our Village_ was a major influence on Gaskell but not on Eliot. “Mitford was, in all likelihood, one of those writers for whom Eliot could only express, as she put it in her letter to Gaskell, a ‘vague gratitude’ for having ‘charmed’ her,” Morrison writes. “By contrast, _Cranford_ quickened her abilities as a novelist.”    

##<em>Cranford</em> Chapters 12-15 Versus “Janet’s Rependance”

The third and final contest is a blowout. The charming nostalgia of _Cranford_ is now wearing teeth-grittingly thin, and the mechanics behind her tearjerking devices are now sloppily visible. Over the course of _Cranford,_ I count at least a dozen episodes designed to induce the reader to waterworks, from the deaths of Captain Brown and his daughter to Peter’s disappearance, brought on by a sorrowful beating, only to return in the maudlin conclusion.

Eliot, on the other hand, disarms the reader in the opening pages of “Janet’s Repentance” with one of the wittiest exchanges in her canon. The villainous Dempster asserts with ignorant confidence that Presbyterianism, a term derived from the Greek word for “elder,” was founded by one John Presbyter, “a miserable fanatic who wore a suit of leather and went about from town to village … inoculating the vulgar with the asinine virus of Dissent.”

The laughter soon dissolves into devastating social commentary as Dempster turns out to be a callous domestic abuser. Whereas Gaskell spends her final pages on yet more transparent emotional manipulation, Eliot earns every tear with intense psychological authenticity and, in the tale of Janet’s harrowing escape from Demptster, a moving demonstration of the practical, life-altering power of the very Christina faith she bravely rejected when she was 17 years old, at the cost of her father’s love. Gaskell draws on teenage heartache as well—the disappearance of her brother John Stevenson on his way to India when she was 18 is clearly the model for Poor Peter—but the regurgitation of private pain in a context where every chapter contains an attempt to wring tears ends up feeling hollow, even exploitative. By contrast, Eliot channels the break with her father and the grief she felt when her brother severed all contact over her relationship with Lewes, which occurred just weeks before she wrote “Janet’s Repentance,” into the defiant compassion that runs through all three stories in _Scenes of a Clerical Life._  

No one saw those distinctions more clearly than Mrs. Gaskell herself. Looking back at her own accomplishments at the height of her success in 1863, Gaskell wrote “I have a feeling that it is not worth while trying to write, while there are such books as Adam Bede and Scenes of a Clerical Life — I set 'Janet's Repentance' above all, still.” Perhaps it was a consolation then to have received Eliot’s letter in 1859, to know that one of her slightest works, _Cranford,_ had been rendered with enough vitality and truth to unlock the towering potential within Eliot that the world had never before seen, and the peculiar, singular character of which the world has never seen again.
